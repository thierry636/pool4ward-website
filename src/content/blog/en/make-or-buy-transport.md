Ask the question in a logistics committee. The answer comes quickly: *because it isn't our core business.* That is a reasonable answer. It is not a decision — it is an inheritance. And the conditions that produced it are all shifting at once.

In most of the companies we meet, the **make or buy** call on transport has not been replayed in fifteen, twenty, sometimes thirty years. That is not a reproach: when a choice works, you don't reopen it. But it is worth looking at where it came from.

## It isn't a doctrine, it's a cycle

Nothing in economic theory says you must outsource your transport. What it says — since Coase and Williamson — is more nuanced: you hand to the market what is commoditised, and you keep in-house what is specific, uncertain and frequent. The diesel truck was commoditised. The conclusion followed.

Three facts tipped the French curve: transport capacity that was abundant and lastingly cheap, the doctrine of refocusing on the core business, and an accounting advantage — taking trucks off the balance sheet mechanically improves return on capital employed.

None of the three is a law. They are **drivers**. And a driver is something you update.

## The five drivers, and where they stand

<!-- COMPONENT: numbered list "drivers", each item has a number, a title, a "Yesterday" line and a "Today" line, figures in mono -->

**01 — The cost of capacity**
*Yesterday:* abundant capacity, prices under permanent pressure. Buying cost less than producing.
*Today:* driver shortage, costs indexed to public indices. The gap is narrowing.

**02 — The balance sheet**
*Yesterday:* moving assets off the books improves return on capital employed.
*Today:* unchanged. It is the one driver that has not moved — and often the only one still genuinely solid.

**03 — Asset specificity**
*Yesterday:* a diesel truck is interchangeable, resellable, unconnected to your sites.
*Today:* an electric truck is coupled to a depot, a charger, a connected power capacity. The asset becomes specific again.

**04 — Data and proof**
*Yesterday:* transport is an invisible service: it arrives, or it doesn't.
*Today:* CSRD, scope 3, ISO 14083. You are no longer buying only a movement, you are buying a proof — and it is built in the operation.

**05 — The commitment horizon**
*Yesterday:* a 12 to 24 month contract was enough to run a diesel fleet.
*Today:* the electric asset is amortised over 6 to 8 years. Your carrier cannot finance on your horizon.

## Electrification, the driver nobody put in the equation

This is the one we find most underestimated. A diesel truck is a variable cost backed by a liquid asset. An electric truck is something else: a vehicle, a charger, a connected power capacity, a charging curve, an energy contract. Cost shifts from variable to fixed — and two consequences follow.

**A mismatch in duration, first.** The TCO of electric drops below diesel, but under conditions: high mileage, subsidies maintained, charging available. And above all, somebody has to carry the investment over six to eight years against a contract that runs two. Your carrier will not do it — or will bill you for the risk.

**A reversal of position, second.** What the electric truck demands — land, a roof, connected power, an overnight charging window — is what you have, not your carrier. Hence a question that did not exist ten years ago: *could you supply the energy without operating the trucks?*

Without getting carried away, though. A 44-tonne electric truck consumes on the order of 130 MWh a year: the equivalent of 600 to 700 m² of solar panels — for a single truck, and produced in daytime when charging happens at night. Self-generation does not make you autonomous. It makes you a link in the energy chain. That is already a considerable reversal.

## Make, buy… and the box everyone forgets

The debate is badly framed as long as it stays binary. There are four building blocks, and nothing forces you to decide them together:

| Block | The question | Who is best placed |
|---|---|---|
| Energy | Generate, store, charge | Often you — land, roof, grid connection |
| Asset | Carry the capital and the residual value | A lessor, or you if the commitment is long |
| Operation | Driving, scheduling, labour, regulation | The carrier, almost always |
| **Volume risk** | **Absorbing peaks and troughs** | **Neither one: several shippers together** |

On that last line, neither *make* nor *buy* answers. That is exactly where pooling between shippers sits — the intermediate form, neither all market nor all in-house. And its role changes in nature: it no longer serves only to gain a few points of fill rate, it becomes what makes the asset financeable, because it aggregates commitments nobody can carry alone.

## Five questions to put to your own team

<!-- COMPONENT: "questions" box, ordered list, accent rule on the left -->

> None of them has an obvious answer. That is the point.
>
> 1. When did we last *calculate* our transport call, rather than inherit it?
> 2. What share of our transport cost buys capacity, and what share buys data and compliance?
> 3. Who carries the electric asset risk in our chain — and have we already paid for it without seeing it?
> 4. Do our sites have land, roof space, connected power? Have we ever linked them to our transport strategy?
> 5. What horizon can we commit to? If the answer is "one year", we will not decarbonise.

We do not think shippers should bring their transport back in-house. We think the question deserves to be reopened — and that the *make / buy* pair is no longer enough to frame it.
