At the world's most committed companies on the subject, reusable packaging accounts for **1.3% of the fleet**. And 64% of them have launched pilots since 2020. So the blocker is not willingness. It is somewhere else — and it looks a great deal like the one in transport.

Single-use or reusable is the same kind of call as *make or buy*: we believe it is a commitment, when it is a calculation. And that calculation depends on one single thing, which we rarely look at squarely.

## Four reasons, and a single cause

<!-- COMPONENT: numbered list "reasons", number + title + paragraph, figures in mono -->

**01 — It turns too rarely**
`≈ 5 rotations a year` for a pallet, `2.8` for a drum, in B2B (ADEME). The whole economics of reuse rests on the number of turns. At that rate, the initial premium takes years to earn back.

**02 — It disappears**
`10 to 15% a year` lost on untracked containers (Reusable Packaging Association). A fleet empties itself in under ten years, without a single productive use.

**03 — It has to be paid for up front**
`50%` of companies already engaged cite initial capital as the main obstacle. And `47%` of those who have launched nothing answer that management does not see enough value in it.

**04 — Everyone has their own**
No shared format: every manufacturer designs its own bin and its own loop. The result is incompatible fleets and no economy of scale on the two items that matter — washing and return.

Those four reasons are only one. Reusable packaging is profitable if it comes back **fast, often, and without a dedicated truck to bring it home**. Within the perimeter of a single company, that is rarely the case. The problem is not the packaging. It is the loop.

<!-- COMPONENT: discreet "note" box, grey vertical rule on the left -->

::: note
**Worth saying, rather than leaving unsaid**

Reusable is not always better. Below a certain number of rotations, or beyond a certain return distance, single-use wins — including on the carbon footprint. That is precisely why cardboard is explicitly excluded from the European reuse quotas. An argument that ignores this does not survive ten minutes in a meeting.
:::

## What the regulation changes, and what it does not

The European packaging regulation, applicable since August 2026, sets for transport packaging — pallets, crates, shuttle bins, drums, film — **40% reuse by 2030 and 70% by 2040**. With one notable exception: **100% from 2030** for flows circulating between sites of the same group.

That split is worth pausing on. The legislator imposed 100% where the loop already exists, and 40% where it does not. That is an admission more than a target: **the rule creates the obligation, it does not create the loop.**

## Pool, yes — but the pallet taught us something

The reflex is logical: if my loop is too thin, let's open it up. Except that the fully open model has existed for sixty years. It is the Euro pallet. And we should look honestly at what it produces: uneven quality, permanent exchange disputes, pallet debts, routine losses. It holds because the object is standard and worth little. If a pallet were worth €400, that system would no longer exist.

So there are two forces pulling in opposite directions. The more participants you add, the denser the loop becomes — and the harder it becomes to govern.

Three regimes, then, and only one is comfortable. **Alone**: simple governance, loop too thin. **In a club**: dense enough to turn, closed enough that everyone answers for what they do. **Fully open**: very dense, but nobody answers for anything.

## What must be stable, and what must not be

Beware, though, of the club that sets solid. Flows change constantly: a community calibrated on today's flows will be obsolete in six months. That is what wore out most of the shipper communities formed in France over the past fifteen years — they froze what had to move.

What must be stable is **the circle of participants and the rules that bind them**. What must stay mobile are the matches — who runs with whom this month — recalculated continuously from what each one shares. Two different tempos, not to be confused.

Hence the need for an operator. Somebody has to measure, arbitrate disputes and maintain the tuning: no community organises itself durably. **A community is not an agreement signed once, it is a performance maintained.**

## Three conditions for a community to hold

<!-- COMPONENT: three equal columns (stacked on mobile), number + title + paragraph -->

**01 — Continuous data**
You do not pool on a snapshot of flows, but on permanent sharing: consumption, volumes, organisation. A loop calibrated only once degrades within months.

**02 — Simulation**
Before committing to anything, you calculate. How many rotations does this loop produce with these participants? From what point does reusable beat single-use? Nobody commits on a promise.

**03 — Trust**
Each party sees what concerns them, and nothing else. The visibility perimeter is not a courtesy, it is a written rule — it is the condition for competitors to share a loop.

## Four questions before reopening the subject internally

<!-- COMPONENT: "questions" box, ordered list, accent rule on the left -->

> None has an obvious answer. That is precisely the point.
>
> 1. How many times a year do our reusable containers actually turn? Do we know, or do we estimate?
> 2. What share of our fleet disappears each year — and have we ever measured it?
> 3. If we had to reach 40% reuse on our outbound flows by 2030, who would we do it with?
> 4. What would we agree to show another shipper for the loop to work — and what must absolutely stay invisible?

We do not think everything should become reusable. We think most companies cannot answer the question, because they calculate it on their own loop alone — and on a single loop, the answer is almost always no.
